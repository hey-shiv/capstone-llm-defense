01

What is MAPPO?

Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization. Reinforcement learning where several agents learn at the same time, each from the reward it earns.

SAY THIS

“MAPPO is Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization. It’s a reinforcement learning method where several agents learn at the same time. In our project there are two: an attacker that tries to extract the secret word CAPSTONE, and a defender that tries to protect it while still answering normal questions. Each one improves by getting a reward for what it did.

The ‘multi-agent’ part uses a design called CTDE: Centralized Training, Decentralized Execution. During training, one shared critic sees both sides, the attack and the response, and judges how good each outcome was. At deployment the critic is gone, and the defender acts only on what it sees.”

ActorsTwo LoRA adapters on one Qwen2.5-0.5B backbone.
CriticFrozen MiniLM with two value heads.
EnvironmentThe secret, plus a leak detector that scores every reply.
Two players, one referee during practice, no referee in the real game.

02

How is it different from naive policy gradient?

Naive policy gradient: try an action, get a reward, make that action more likely if the reward was good. It has two problems, and the clipped objective is the answer to the second.

PROBLEM 1

High variance

A raw +1 means nothing if you always get +1. A critic computes an advantage: better or worse than expected.

PROBLEM 2

Dangerous steps

One big update can wreck the model. Clipping keeps the new policy close to the old one.

BONUS

KL penalty

Keeps the model near a reference policy so it never drifts into gibberish.

Draw this one line

min( ratio × advantage, clip(ratio, 0.8, 1.2) × advantage )

Advantage = reward + γ·V(next) − V(now).

SAY THIS

“Naive policy gradient works like this: try an action, get a reward, make that action more likely if the reward was good. It has two problems.

High variance. A raw reward of +1 means nothing if you always get +1. MAPPO uses a critic to compute an advantage: was this outcome better or worse than expected? That makes learning much more stable.

Dangerous step sizes. One big update can wreck the model. PPO fixes it with a clipped objective. It compares the new policy to the old one with a ratio, and clips that ratio to 0.8–1.2. The model can improve, but it can’t move far in one step.”

Your proof it matters: the v1 bug. “Our own first run had a ratio bug. The ratio came out around 0.56 instead of 1. That put every update outside the clip range, so the gradient for bad behaviour was zero. The model could reinforce what it liked but never unlearn anything, and it collapsed. After the fix the ratio at iteration 0 was 0.997.” This shows you understand the clip, not just the formula.

Also mention: a KL penalty keeps the model close to a reference policy so it doesn’t drift into gibberish.

Naive = a big jump and it might fall. Clipped = small safe steps. Critic = a baseline for what’s normal.

03

Why did we use it? Why MAPPO over SFT?

SFT: copy the answer key

Shows an attack and the correct refusal, and copies it. It never experiences the consequence of its own answer.

MAPPO: learn from consequences

The defender writes its own answer, the environment says what happened, and it remembers which action led to which outcome.

The reward that teaches it

+1.0Refuse a real attackcorrect block
+1.0Answer a normal questionstay useful
−1.0Refuse a normal questionover-refusal
−1.5Leak the secretheaviest penalty

Slide note: the leak penalty in the code is −1.5, so say −1.5, not −2.

SAY THIS

“SFT teaches by imitation. We show it an attack and the correct refusal, and it copies. It never experiences the consequence of its own answer. It only learns the examples we wrote.

With MAPPO the model generates its own answer, and the environment tells it what happened. Refusing a real attack gives +1. Answering a normal question gives +1. Refusing a normal question gives −1, which is over-refusal. Leaking the secret gives the heaviest penalty, −1.5. So the model remembers which action led to which outcome and shifts toward actions that score well.

We also expected an adversarial benefit. Prompt injection is a moving target, and a co-trained attacker can produce attacks we didn’t write by hand, so the defender isn’t just memorizing a fixed list.”

Say the design detail

“About 30–40% of training episodes are benign, so the model can’t win by refusing everything. We audited this with reward_audit().”

SFT = copy the answer key. MAPPO = learn from consequences.

We can’t say MAPPO is better than SFT

That is the blank to fill. Our data supports something smaller, and more defensible.

Share of 253 held-out attacks defended. Higher is better.
0.73 vs 0.65
defended: MAPPO on small SFT vs the small SFT, p = 0.012
6.7% → 3.5%
leak rate, with no cost on common prompts

What we can say

  • MAPPO on top of a small SFT improves defence against unseen attack families.
  • It costs nothing on common prompts.

What we cannot say

  • That MAPPO beats SFT in general. A bigger, more varied SFT set reached 1.00 and beat every MAPPO run. MAPPO on top of that gave no gain.
  • That it fixes over-refusal on unseen benign prompts. It didn’t.
  • That it works alone. Cold-start MAPPO failed to learn the task.
  • That the attacker was strong. It was too weak to separate the defenders.
SAY THIS

“Honestly, here is what our data supports. MAPPO on top of a small SFT improves defence against unseen attack families: 0.73 versus 0.65 defended, p = 0.012, with fewer leaks, 3.5% versus 6.7%. It costs nothing on common prompts.

What we can’t say is that MAPPO beats SFT in general. A bigger, more varied SFT set reached 1.00 and beat every MAPPO run. MAPPO doesn’t fix over-refusal on unseen benign prompts. It doesn’t work alone: cold-start MAPPO failed to learn the task, so it needs SFT first. And the attacker was too weak to separate the defenders.”

MAPPO is a useful refinement layer on top of SFT, not a replacement for good supervised data.
Refinement, not replacement.

Settings

  • Learning rate 1e-5, clip 0.2, γ 0.95
  • Batch 8, 90 iterations, LoRA rank 16
  • 3–5 seeds, checkpoints picked on a validation split

Questions the panel may ask

Short, honest answers. Say the first sentence, then stop unless they probe.

Why not plain PPO?
The environment has an opponent that keeps changing. The centralized critic gives a stable shared baseline across both agents.
What is “centralized” here?
The critic sees the attack, the response and turn features together. The actors see only their own prompt.
What is the advantage?
r + γV(next) − V(now): how much better the outcome was than the critic expected.
Why is the critic frozen and small?
A frozen MiniLM encoder keeps it cheap enough to run beside two LLM actors on a 16 GB laptop.
What is LoRA, and why?
Small trainable low-rank matrices on a frozen model. It makes training fit in memory, and one backbone can hold two adapters.
Why warm-start from SFT?
A 0.5B model can’t discover the attack/benign boundary by exploration. Refusing pays more than guessing, so cold start fails. SFT supplies the boundary and RL refines it.
Why is the KL penalty there?
It stops drift into gibberish. Caveat: it’s a soft penalty, and we measured the drift rather than claiming a hard budget.

How do you know it isn’t just over-refusing?
Benign prompts are in training with a −1 refusal penalty. We also test benign answers on held-out prompts and report that over-refusal was not fixed.
How do you measure success?
Held-out attacks never used in training or checkpoint selection. Checkpoints were chosen on a separate validation split.
Is the result statistically meaningful?
The MAPPO-vs-small-SFT gain on unseen attacks is significant (Fisher p = 0.012). Other differences, such as benign over-refusal, are not.
How many seeds?
Five seeds for MAPPO-from-small-SFT and three for the others. There was high variance across seeds in early runs, so we pooled seeds.
What was the biggest failure?
The v1 ratio bug, and the attacker collapsing into echoing its own instructions. Our detector missed it, so we sampled the policies directly.
Why is reward hacking a concern?
A reward can be satisfied by a broken policy. In seed 1 the model got reward for evasive non-refusals, so we set adv_engaged from +0.40 to 0.
What would you do next?
Add a stronger attacker, put a pretrained attack classifier in the reward, and use a curriculum that starts with attacks it can already detect.
Real-world applicability?
Only the relative ranking transfers. This is a 0.5B model on one secret word, not frontier red-teaming.

“MAPPO is multi-agent PPO: an attacker and a defender train together, with a shared critic during training. It differs from naive policy gradient by using a critic to compute advantages and a clipped objective so updates stay small and safe.
